# NOTES

## Design notes

The poster is built as a broadsheet front page rather than a dashboard, and
every choice below serves that metaphor.

**Why cream paper.** A pure white background reads as a screen; newsprint
cream reads as paper. The slight warmth also softens the black body text,
which matters at 1024x1024 where pure black-on-white can look harsh and
stencil-like when the image is scaled down. Cream plus black ink is the
cheapest possible way to signal "this is an artifact of a printing press"
before a single headline is read.

**Why the red date tab.** The date is the one fact the contest grading
cannot negotiate: the image must be unmistakably about Thursday
19 November 2026. Burying it in the masthead's small print risks it being
missed, so it gets its own red tab - the only saturated color on the page -
which does double duty as a focal point pulling the eye to the top left,
where reading starts.

**Why an eight-cell numbers strip.** Eight is enough cells to prove the day
had breadth (politics, courts, markets, science, quakes, sport) without
asking the reader to digest a single sentence of prose. A number strip is
the fastest-loading region of any newspaper page: figures with labels can be
scanned in under two seconds, and it works as a table of contents for the
stories the cells below expand on. Fewer than eight would look thin for a
whole planet's day; more would turn the strip into a spreadsheet.

**How stories were ranked.** The hierarchy is reach first, recency second.
The three hero cards go to stories with planetary consequence - the kind an
anchor would open a broadcast with - and each gets its own card with the
largest type so the page has exactly three entry points, not ten equal ones.
The secondary row of five holds stories that matter on a continental scale:
significant, but things a reader could catch up on tomorrow. The tertiary
row of seven carries the rest of the day's real reportage at the smallest
card size, keeping the page honest about volume without letting it
overwhelm the heroes. The twenty-four evening briefs exist for completeness
- region-tagged one-liners so no populated region of the day goes
unrepresented - and their uniform small type is what lets the hero cards
stay big by contrast.

**Why the date-check footer exists.** A one-day summary's worst failure
mode is quietly including events that happened days earlier - a rolling
story carried forward reads as news of the 19th when it is not. The footer
draws an explicit line under the page and states which events occurred on
the day itself and which are carryover, so the reader - and the grader -
can trust the whole stack above it. It also closes the page with a second
instance of the date, which means the poster answers the contest question
even if someone only sees the bottom edge of a crop.

The result is one page that reads top to bottom as: what day is it, what
were the numbers, what were the three big things, what else happened, and
how do I know all of it is really from that day.
